# Will the overall claim that low flow showers cause more net water use hold up thru mid 2026?

## Finding

The claim came from a 3-page policy briefing by Pereira-Doel, Daly and Walker, posted on OSF Preprints on 2024-03-07: across 290 University of Surrey showers (86,421 showers over 39 weeks), higher water pressure went with less water per shower, including at similar flow rates. It did not find that low-flow showers use more water; Walker said low flow rates deliver less water and "the best of all worlds is high pressure, low flow," although the briefing did say higher pressure, "usually associated with higher flow rates, might lead to overall lower water consumption," and that low-flow equipment "should not be the sole focus." By the 2026-06-30 deadline the briefing was still an unreviewed preprint with no journal version, and I found no published replication, rebuttal, or academic exchange about it. The flow-rate evidence (Gauley and Koeller's 50,000-shower analysis, a 2016 Hong Kong study, EPA WaterSense, and an unpublished May 2026 draft of a randomized Finnish trial) says restricting flow lowers total use, while other work finds spray intensity and pressure change shower length; together that is the market's "it varies" case at most. The NO condition requires "a serious academic debate with published and in unbiased studies reversing this," which did not happen, and the criteria give YES both for "no studies" and for "it varies/it's unclear."

## Evidence

It states the pressure finding, says low-flow equipment "should not be the sole focus," quotes the Bathroom Manufacturers Association warning about "efforts to perpetually lower flow," credits Mira Showers, Waterwise, the BMA and Aguardio for feedback or timers, and says the work "is currently in preparation for its publication in a peer-reviewed journal." The [OSF API record](https://api.osf.io/v2/preprints/hxaey/) lists no linked journal article. The March 22 revision raised per-shower volumes (low pressure, no timer: 60.64 to 71.49 L; high pressure with timer: 16.90 to 30.39 L) and cut the timer effect from "up to 53%" to "up to 43%"; the pressure effect remained (71.49 vs 31.62 L without a timer).

- [Adeyeye, She & Meireles, "Beyond the flow rate," Environmental Science and Pollution Research](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7028815/) (published online 2019-12-30): spray intensity, distribution and heat loss affect shower experience and duration; recommends standards consider spray intensity (pressure) as well as flow.
- [EPA WaterSense, Showerheads](https://www.epa.gov/watersense/showerheads) (accessed 2026-09-29): the average family could save 2,700 gallons a year with WaterSense showerheads, which must also meet a minimum spray force ("pressure") requirement.
- [Albertazzi, Leni & Bilancini, "Beyond the Margin: Targeted Conservation and Household Water Demand"](https://iris.imtlucca.it/retrieve/7b2111be-b006-4001-bcd3-b1f7bc7b1e09/Beyond%20the%20Margin_V1.pdf) (PDF dated 2026-05-22, marked "very preliminary draft"; [pre-registration](https://doi.org/10.17605/OSF.IO/BJVZD)): randomized experiment in 775 Finnish households; a water-saving shower head with a flow controller set near 9 L/min (replacing about 12 L/min fixtures) cut daily household water use by about 5%, and the savings showed up on the household meter rather than being offset elsewhere. Not peer-reviewed and did not test pressure.
- [Leong, Buurman & Tay, "Two steps forward, one step back," Behavioural Public Policy](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/behavioural-public-policy/article/two-steps-forward-one-step-back-negative-spillovers-in-water-conservation/F72BF7C266515FC01DD5E19C23700263) (published online 2024-05-22): the Cambridge study linked in the comments; in Singapore, smart-shower feedback raised other household water use by 2.5% but net use still fell 4.7%. It is about feedback devices, not flow rate or pressure.

## Other side

The strongest NO case, made by NevikEcho: the title's claim, that low-flow showers cause more net water use, was never supported. The study's own authors said low flow uses less water, co-author Daly said their data never reached the point where lowering flow stops helping, published flow-rate studies (Gauley and Koeller; Wong, Mui and Zhou) show lower flow means lower total volume with little extra shower time, and the May 2026 Finnish trial found a water-saving shower head cut net household use by about 5%. Read literally, the claim did not hold up, and the market closed at 33%, which suggests many bettors read it that way. This does not change the recommendation because the criteria set a specific bar for NO, a serious academic debate with published, unbiased studies reversing the claim, and nothing met it. The flow-rate studies predate the claim and were not a response to it, the Finnish trial is an unpublished draft that did not test pressure, and no one published a rebuttal or failed replication of the pressure finding. The criteria also say "it varies" means YES, and the combined evidence (flow limits save water on average; pressure and spray quality affect how long people shower) is that case. N/A is arguable on the grounds that the market misread a pressure finding as a flow claim, but I do not recommend it: the flow version did circulate (the briefing's "usually associated with higher flow rates" line, the industry warning against lowering flow, Freethink's "could backfire" framing), and the written criteria can be applied.

## Before resolving

Decide what "the overall claim" means to you. If it is the study's finding that stronger, higher-pressure showers lead to shorter showers and less water, nothing published reversed it and YES follows from "If no studies YES." If you meant the literal title, that low-flow showers use more water in total, the evidence says that claim was never true, which supports NO in spirit; but your criteria ask for a published academic reversal, and the only new study against it is an unreviewed draft. I lean YES under the text as written.
